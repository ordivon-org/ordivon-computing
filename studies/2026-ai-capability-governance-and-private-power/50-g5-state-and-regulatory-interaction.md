## Core finding

The relevant structure is rarely:

```text
State constrains Provider
```

It is more often:

```text
State law and strategy
+ Provider policy and contract
+ cloud / compute chokepoints
+ identity and region systems
+ delegated monitoring and reporting
+ courts, regulators and appeals
→ hybrid capability governance
```

Public law can create counter-power through explanation, documentation, audit,
researcher access, judicial review, and regulator oversight. It can also increase
centralization through compliance cost, licensing, identity requirements,
compute control, national-security exceptions, and delegated private enforcement.

## Key tensions

### Public reason-giving versus private opacity

The EU DSA statement-of-reasons system demonstrates that law can require
standardized explanation and public aggregate visibility. It does not guarantee
that every AI capability decision receives technically meaningful evidence or an
independent merits review.

### Systemic-risk duties versus incumbent advantage

EU AI Act duties can force evaluation, documentation, incident reporting, and
cybersecurity that voluntary Provider systems may underproduce. The same duties
require legal, technical, and administrative capacity that smaller or open actors
may lack.

### National security versus user exit

US export, infrastructure, and national-security policies can distribute American
AI capability to allies while limiting access elsewhere and binding cloud, chip,
and model Providers into geopolitical governance.

### Filing and service registration versus direct political administration

China's registration and algorithm/service rules create visible administrative
identities and product obligations. They also place model and application access
inside a state-supervised content, data, identity, and platform distribution
system.

### Sovereignty versus openness

Canada's 2026 national strategy emphasizes trust, opportunity, and sovereign AI
infrastructure. Sovereignty can improve domestic exit from foreign Providers, but
may also justify centralized procurement, preferred suppliers, and state-selected
infrastructure.

## G5 disposition

Retain a two-sided regulatory test:

```text
Counter-power effect
  Does the rule create reasons, evidence, review, remedy, portability, or public
  participation that constrains Provider discretion?

Consolidation effect
  Does the rule create compliance, identity, compute, procurement, or monitoring
  barriers that strengthen incumbents or state-provider coalitions?
```

No regulation should be classified as simply restrictive or liberating without
both analyses.
