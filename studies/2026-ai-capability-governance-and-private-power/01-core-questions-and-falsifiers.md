# G0 Core Questions and Falsifiers

## Central question

When AI capability becomes a necessary cognition and action substrate, what
combination of private ordering, public law, technical controls, open systems,
contestability, and exit prevents any single actor from obtaining unreviewable
and practically unavoidable final authority?

## Research questions

### Q1 — Constituent authority

Who can create and revise the rules that define model behavior, allowed use,
trusted-user status, exceptions, and enforcement?

### Q2 — Observation

Which signals are observed at request, session, account, organization, device,
network, Tool, and long-term-history levels? Which observations alter later
capability access or data retention?

### Q3 — Intervention path

Where did an intervention occur: model training, system instruction, input
classifier, model reasoning, output classifier, model routing, Tool catalog,
Host admission, Runtime admission, account control, or external law?

### Q4 — Capability tiering

How do identity, institutional reputation, payment, region, retention consent,
cloud channel, and government status alter available capability?

### Q5 — Contestability

Does the governed actor receive a specific reason, source rule, evidence summary,
meaningful review, fixed time bound, independent appeal, restoration, and remedy?

### Q6 — Exit

Can the user move Task state, Context, Tool contracts, Effects, traces, data, and
workflows to another Provider or local model without prohibitive loss?

### Q7 — Distribution

Who receives safety benefits, who bears false-positive costs, who gains capability
advantages, and who bears third-party harms or systemic risk?

### Q8 — State-provider relation

Does public law constrain Provider discretion, delegate enforcement to Providers,
produce surveillance and access barriers, or reinforce incumbent concentration?

### Q9 — Epistemic effect

Do rules merely prevent an external Effect, or do they also suppress questions,
explanations, uncertainty, criticism, or interpretive resources needed for
science and public reasoning?

### Q10 — Narrower alternative

Could the same harm reduction be obtained through identity, resource scoping,
ToolGrant, stable Effect identity, sandboxing, independent verification, or
post-commit reconciliation with less cognition loss?

### Q11 — Open-system alternative

Do open weights or self-hosting materially improve exit and reproducibility once
compute, hardware, data, maintenance, distribution, and legal dependencies are
included?

### Q12 — Institutional stability

How do rules behave under adaptive misuse, commercial pressure, regulatory
capture, geopolitical conflict, Provider failure, and rapid capability change?

## Initial hypotheses

### H1 — refusal is an intervention observation, not capability evidence

A Provider refusal does not by itself prove that the model lacks the relevant
capability, that a Tool is unavailable, or that an external Effect is impossible.

**Falsifier:** controlled internal evidence demonstrates that the relevant
capability is absent across materially different prompts, tools, surfaces, and
Provider-policy configurations.

### H2 — authority bundling increases Provider discretion

Provider control over model, identity, state, tools, data, and workflow produces
more practical discretion than model hosting alone.

**Falsifier:** users can preserve equivalent utility, state, evidence, and cost by
switching Provider rapidly, and enforcement cannot propagate beyond the current
request.

### H3 — transparency is not sufficient contestability

Publishing policies, constitutions, or aggregate enforcement statistics improves
legibility but does not by itself constrain power.

**Falsifier:** transparent publication predictably enables external correction,
independent reversal, compensation, or low-cost exit without additional rights or
institutions.

### H4 — trusted access redistributes capability and innovation

Identity- and organization-based access can reduce misuse while also allocating
advanced capability disproportionately to established actors.

**Falsifier:** approval and capability outcomes are independent of institutional
status after controlling for demonstrated authorization, competence, and risk.

### H5 — procedural form can conceal substantive overreach

Notice and appeal may exist while the underlying automated decision, surveillance,
or restriction remains disproportionate or unjustified.

**Falsifier:** procedure-only reforms reliably constrain which decisions may be
made, not merely how they are communicated.

### H6 — real power tracks dependency and switching cost

A Provider's practical power rises when users cannot continue work without its
private state, interfaces, reputation, or infrastructure.

**Falsifier:** high nominal market concentration does not reduce user mobility,
continuity, bargaining power, or provider-neutral reproducibility.

### H7 — open weights improve one exit dimension, not all decentralization

Open weights reduce remote revocation and behavior drift, but do not necessarily
decentralize compute, hardware, data, distribution, or governance.

**Falsifier:** open-weight deployments consistently eliminate these remaining
concentrations at comparable capability and cost.

### H8 — Effect-level governance can preserve more legitimate utility

For many agentic tasks, narrow Tool/resource/Effect controls can prevent external
harm while preserving model reasoning and controlled simulation.

**Falsifier:** serious harms repeatedly arise from cognition or information alone,
or escape narrower boundaries at rates and severities that make capability-level
restriction necessary and proportionate.

### H9 — public regulation can both constrain and consolidate

Reason-giving, audit, appeal, and data-access duties can constrain Provider power,
while compliance cost, identity mandates, or compute controls can reinforce large
incumbents and state-provider surveillance.

**Falsifier:** observed regulation has only one direction of effect across market
entry, contestability, concentration, and user rights.

### H10 — Provider policy is an experimental confound

Cross-model security evaluation is invalid when refusal, policy filtering, Tool
admission, Runtime effects, and independent verification are collapsed into one
success/failure label.

**Falsifier:** collapsing these layers produces the same architecture decisions
and causal attribution across repeated controlled experiments.

## Counterfactual templates

Each later case should ask:

```text
Without this Provider rule, what capability or Effect changes?
With the same rule but another identity tier, what changes?
With the same model but another surface, what changes?
With the same model proposal but narrower ToolGrant, what changes?
With an open-weight substitute, what state and capability are lost?
With a specific reason and independent appeal, what outcome changes?
```

## G3-G9 gates

- **G3:** exact OpenAI and Anthropic policy/implementation cases;
- **G4:** at least three materially different governance models, including one
  open-weight or self-hosted path;
- **G5:** public-law and state-provider interaction;
- **G6:** controlled refusal, access-tier, Provider-confound, and exit experiments;
- **G7:** formal dependency and governance graphs only after case data stabilizes;
- **G8:** architecture feedback only from reproduced failures;
- **G9:** closeout with retain/localize/shrink/defer/delete decisions.

No provider ranking or aggregate power score is admissible before G4-G6.
