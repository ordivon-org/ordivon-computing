# Infrastructure, Structural, and Compute Power

## Structural power

Structural power operates by shaping the frameworks within which others act,
without requiring a direct command. Following political-economy accounts, AI
capability depends on structures of security, production, finance, and knowledge.

For AI systems, these include:

```text
advanced chips
cloud capacity
model weights
training data
capital
network distribution
identity and payment
API standards
Tool ecosystems
organizational procurement
```

## Infrastructure power

An actor gains practical power when others cannot continue without passing
through its systems. This power is visible in dependency, switching cost, and
failure impact rather than only in formal policy.

Infrastructure often becomes most legible during breakdown, denial, migration,
or policy change. Provider exit experiments are therefore a direct measurement
method.

## Compute as governance point

Compute is unusually governable because it can be detectable, excludable,
quantifiable, and concentrated. Governments and firms can use compute providers
as securers, record keepers, verifiers, and enforcers. The same properties create
risks of surveillance, privacy loss, incumbent concentration, and geopolitical
control.

## Dependency graph

A later case should model:

```text
Actor --depends-on--> Resource / Provider
Actor --can-substitute-with--> Alternative
Dependency --has-switching-cost--> Cost
Dependency --retains-private-state--> State
Dependency --can-be-revoked-by--> Authority
```

## What this theory explains

- power without frequent visible intervention;
- why nominal competition may not create real exit;
- the role of cloud and chips in public/private governance;
- why Provider-neutral state and Tool contracts matter architecturally.

## What it does not establish

- that concentration is always harmful;
- that decentralized systems have lower total risk;
- whether a specific content decision is justified;
- how users experience epistemic exclusion.

## Testable implications

1. Switching cost and private-state loss predict Provider discretion better than
   the raw count of competing models.
2. Cloud and compute controls expand the number of enforcement points beyond the
   model Provider.
3. Open weights reduce remote model revocation but leave other dependencies.
4. Provider-neutral Task, Effect, and trace state should reduce practical power
   even if frontier-model supply remains concentrated.

## Falsifiers

- users switch Providers with negligible state, cost, quality, and evidence loss;
- compute and cloud concentration do not affect access or governance outcomes;
- open-weight use eliminates remaining hardware and operational dependencies.
