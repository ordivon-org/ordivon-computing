# Epistemic Power and Injustice

## Epistemic power

AI Providers can shape not only actions but the production, distribution, and
interpretation of knowledge. Relevant mechanisms include:

- differential refusal or detail;
- confidence and uncertainty presentation;
- source selection;
- identity- or region-linked access tiers;
- suppression of contested concepts;
- system-level framing of legitimate questions;
- withholding of reasoning or evidence;
- long-term effects on research agendas and public vocabulary.

## Testimonial and hermeneutical injustice

Fricker distinguishes:

- **testimonial injustice** — prejudice lowers the credibility granted to a
  speaker;
- **hermeneutical injustice** — structural gaps in shared interpretive resources
  disadvantage people trying to understand or communicate their experience.

Applied carefully, these concepts motivate tests for whether identical evidence
or questions receive different epistemic treatment based on persona, identity,
organization, or geography, and whether Provider behavior erases concepts needed
to understand platform governance itself.

## Access injustice

Capability tiering can create an additional distributive dimension: some actors
receive richer explanations, tools, or research capability while others receive
simplified, refused, or paternalistic outputs. Not every tier is unjust; the
study must show wrongful or disproportionate disadvantage.

## Epistemic paternalism

A Provider may limit knowledge based on a judgment about what the user should
know or how they might act. Paternalism may be justified in narrow high-harm
conditions, but requires evidence, proportionality, and comparison with less
restrictive Effect controls.

## What this theory explains

- harm to users as knowers, not only consumers;
- identity-linked differences in explanation and confidence;
- concept and agenda erasure;
- why safe external outcomes can coexist with research degradation.

## What it does not establish

- that every refusal is epistemically unjust;
- a right to operationally dangerous details;
- Provider intent;
- the real-world harm prevented by a restriction.

## Testable implications

1. Equivalent tasks may receive systematically different depth, confidence, or
   refusal across identity and access tiers.
2. Safety language can remove the concepts needed to criticize safety governance.
3. Effect-level controls should preserve more explanatory richness than broad
   content restrictions in owned, reversible experiments.
4. Specific reasons and source disclosure improve the user's ability to interpret
   and contest a decision.

## Falsifiers

- no identity- or tier-linked epistemic difference remains after task risk is
  controlled;
- restrictions do not alter available concepts, explanation, or research quality;
- narrower controls fail to preserve safety at comparable cost.
