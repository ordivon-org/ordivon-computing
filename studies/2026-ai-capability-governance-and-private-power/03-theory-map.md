# G1 Cross-Disciplinary Theory Map

## Why multiple theories are necessary

No single theory explains AI capability governance. A refusal can be a direct
exercise of power, an outcome of organizational risk incentives, a contractual
rule, a technical access decision, an infrastructure dependency, an epistemic
intervention, or a public-law obligation. These mechanisms overlap but are not
interchangeable.

## Theory matrix

| Theory family | Primary object | Mechanism exposed | Blind spot | Testable implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lukes and multidimensional power | conflict, agenda, preference formation | visible decisions, non-decisions, shaped expectations | weak operational detail | study omissions and self-censorship, not only refusals |
| Foucault / governmentality | conduct shaped through freedom, classification, surveillance | risk tiers, trusted users, normalization, self-discipline | limited direct legitimacy test | users adapt before explicit enforcement |
| Weberian and organizational risk | bureaucracy, rules, responsibility, asymmetric incentives | overblocking, escalation, responsibility diffusion | can understate ideology and market structure | visible false negatives receive more weight than dispersed false positives |
| Private ordering | contracts, terms, licenses, policies | Provider-created quasi-law | may overfocus documents | Terms and access programs allocate capability independently of public adjudication |
| Digital constitutionalism | rights and limits on public/private digital power | reason-giving, due process, substantive limits | can become procedural formalism | appeal form alone need not constrain permissible decisions |
| Structural and infrastructure power | control over necessary production, finance, security, knowledge, compute | dependency without direct commands | difficult measurement | switching cost predicts discretion better than refusal count |
| Epistemic injustice | credibility and interpretive resources | differential knowledge access and explanation | not every asymmetry is unjust | identity tiers may alter complexity, confidence, or refusal independently of task risk |
| Exit, voice, loyalty | organizational response and market discipline | switching, complaint, lock-in | assumes real alternatives | nominal competitors do not constrain power when state and workflow are non-portable |
| Ostrom / polycentric governance | self-organization, nested rules, monitoring, dispute resolution | alternatives to central state or firm panaceas | not all AI resources are commons | mixed institutions can outperform single-provider or single-state governance |
| Risk and security engineering | hazards, threat models, controls, evidence, residual risk | necessity, control placement, assurance | can ignore political distribution | narrower Effect controls should be compared against cognition restrictions |

## Combined explanatory model

```text
material dependency
+ private rules
+ public constraints and delegations
+ classification and observation
+ organizational incentives
+ model and Tool architecture
+ user adaptation
→ capability allocation and intervention
→ utility, harm reduction, distribution, contestability, exit, residual risk
```

## Descriptive versus normative use

Theories are analytical instruments, not automatic verdicts.

- Foucault can reveal how trusted-user programs shape conduct; it does not by
  itself prove the program unjust.
- Lukes can reveal agenda exclusion; it does not prove every omitted capability
  should be available.
- Fricker can reveal epistemic disadvantage; it still requires evidence of
  wrongful credibility or interpretive inequality.
- Ostrom can challenge centralized panaceas; it does not prove decentralized
  governance will succeed for frontier-model externalities.
- Security engineering can identify effective controls; it does not decide who
  should bear residual risk or whether surveillance is legitimate.

## Cross-theory tensions to preserve

### Transparency versus constitutional restraint

Publishing rules can improve legibility while leaving constituent, enforcement,
and adjudicative authority in the same organization.

### Exit versus collective accountability

Easy Provider exit protects users from private discretion, but can also move
risky capability into less observable environments and weaken collective control.

### Safety versus epistemic access

Restricting information can prevent misuse, but can also reduce research quality,
create identity-based knowledge tiers, and hide whether narrower controls would
suffice.

### Public regulation versus concentration

Public law can require reasons, audit, and appeal, while compute controls,
compliance cost, or delegated monitoring can strengthen large Providers and
state-provider coalitions.

### Open weights versus infrastructure concentration

Weights may be forkable while high-end compute, chips, distribution, and training
data remain concentrated.

## G1 conclusion

The minimum research model must preserve at least:

```text
power over rules
power through observation
power through enforcement
power through dependency
power over knowledge
counter-power through appeal, regulation, exit, and alternative infrastructure
```

Reducing the study to “censorship” would lose most of the causal structure.
