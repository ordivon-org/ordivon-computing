# Source Ledger

The ledger records what each source can and cannot support. Historical analogy is never treated as proof that AI outcomes must repeat the past.

## Industrial history and general-purpose technology

### H01 — Steam power and manufacturing productivity

- Source: Jeremy Atack, Fred Bateman, and Robert Margo, “Steam Power, Establishment Size, and Labor Productivity Growth in Nineteenth Century American Manufacturing,” NBER Working Paper 11931, 2006; published in *Explorations in Economic History*, 2008.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w11931>
- Supports: steam-powered establishments had higher labor productivity; gains varied by establishment size; steam diffusion contributed materially to manufacturing productivity growth.
- Limitation: U.S. manufacturing evidence from 1850–1880 does not establish a universal law of technological inevitability.

### H02 — Corliss steam engine as a general-purpose technology

- Source: Nathan Rosenberg and Manuel Trajtenberg, “A General Purpose Technology at Work: The Corliss Steam Engine in the Late 19th Century US,” NBER Working Paper 8485, 2001.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w8485>
- Supports: steam relaxed geographic constraints imposed by waterpower and enabled wider industrial location choices.
- Limitation: one engine design and historical context cannot establish the pace of AI diffusion.

### H03 — Reinterpreting Luddism

- Source: Adrian Randall, “Reinterpreting ‘Luddism’: Resistance to New Technology in the British Industrial Revolution,” in *Resistance to New Technology*, Cambridge University Press, 1995.
- URL: <https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/resistance-to-new-technology/reinterpreting-luddism-resistance-to-new-technology-in-the-british-industrial-revolution/B3E8AE7F9B38458CB3AA67814361F2FA>
- Supports: Luddism should not be reduced to irrational technophobia; resistance was embedded in labor relations, distress, bargaining, and control over production.
- Limitation: the chapter does not imply that every modern technology critic is a disguised incumbent interest.

### H04 — British attempts to prevent technology outflow

- Source: David J. Jeremy, “Damming the Flood: British Government Efforts to Check the Outflow of Technicians and Machinery, 1780–1843,” *Business History Review* 51(1), 1977.
- URL: <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/damming-the-flood-british-government-efforts-to-check-the-outflow-of-technicians-and-machinery-17801843/C2351C757F8433D0B3701BDBF4D1BFB6>
- Supports: prohibitions on machinery exports and skilled-worker emigration became contradictory and difficult to enforce despite sustained state effort.
- Limitation: nineteenth-century enforcement capacity and industrial machinery are not identical to modern compute supply chains or digital surveillance.

### H05 — Electrification and organizational change

- Source: Martin Fiszbein, Jeanne Lafortune, Ethan G. Lewis, and José Tessada, “Powering Up Productivity: The Effects of Electrification on U.S. Manufacturing,” NBER Working Paper 28076, revised 2024.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w28076>
- Supports: electrification produced rapid and persistent productivity gains accompanied by capital deepening and organizational change; effects varied with market structure.
- Limitation: the study does not show that all electrification benefits were immediate or evenly distributed.

### H06 — General-purpose technologies

- Source: Timothy F. Bresnahan and Manuel Trajtenberg, “General Purpose Technologies ‘Engines of Growth?’,” NBER Working Paper 4148, 1992; *Journal of Econometrics*, 1995.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w4148>
- Supports: GPTs are pervasive, improve over time, and create innovation complementarities; decentralized economies may produce too little complementary innovation.
- Limitation: GPT classification does not establish that every powerful technology should be accelerated without constraint.

### H07 — IT, workplace organization, and skilled labor

- Source: Timothy F. Bresnahan, Erik Brynjolfsson, and Lorin M. Hitt, “Information Technology, Workplace Organization and the Demand for Skilled Labor,” NBER Working Paper 7136, 1999.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w7136>
- Supports: IT adoption was complementary with organizational and service innovation and changed skill demand.
- Limitation: firm-level complementarities do not resolve distributional or macroeconomic policy questions.

### H08 — Productivity J-curve

- Source: Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock, and Chad Syverson, “The Productivity J-Curve: How Intangibles Complement General Purpose Technologies,” NBER Working Paper 25148, revised 2020.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148>
- Supports: GPTs require large intangible complementary investments whose costs can precede measured productivity gains.
- Limitation: a J-curve is not guaranteed for every AI application or economy.

### H09 — Broad technology diffusion facts

- Source: Diego Comin, Bart Hobijn, and Emilie Rovito, “Five Facts You Need to Know About Technology Diffusion,” NBER Working Paper 11928, 2006.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w11928>
- Supports: technology adoption varies widely across countries, while newer technologies have converged faster than older ones.
- Limitation: historical diffusion averages do not predict frontier-model diffusion under modern export controls.

### H10 — Education, trade openness, and adoption

- Source: Diego Comin and Bart Hobijn, “An Exploration of Technology Diffusion,” NBER Working Paper 12314, 2006; *American Economic Review*, 2010.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w12314>
- Supports: educational attainment and trade openness help explain technology-adoption lags.
- Limitation: the model does not exhaust institutional, political, or security determinants.

### H11 — Lobbies and technology diffusion

- Source: Diego Comin and Bart Hobijn, “Lobbies and Technology Diffusion,” NBER Working Paper 11022, revised 2007.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w11022>
- Supports: incumbent lobbying can materially slow adoption, especially when a new technology has a close predecessor.
- Limitation: some resistance may reflect legitimate externalities rather than rent protection.

### H12 — Technology penetration and income divergence

- Source: Diego Comin and Martí Mestieri, “If Technology Has Arrived Everywhere, Why Has Income Diverged?,” NBER Working Paper 19010, 2013; *AEJ: Macroeconomics*, 2018.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w19010>
- Supports: divergence in technology penetration can explain a large share of long-run income divergence despite convergence in adoption lags.
- Limitation: macroeconomic attribution remains model-dependent.

## Labor, distribution, and adaptation

### L01 — AI, automation, and work

- Source: Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, “Artificial Intelligence, Automation and Work,” NBER Working Paper 24196, 2018.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w24196>
- Supports: automation creates displacement effects that may reduce labor demand and wages, partly counteracted by productivity, capital accumulation, and new tasks.
- Limitation: the framework does not determine the exact future task distribution under advanced AI.

### L02 — Automation and wage inequality

- Source: Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, “Tasks, Automation, and the Rise in U.S. Wage Inequality,” NBER Working Paper 28920, 2021.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w28920>
- Supports: task displacement associated with automation explains a large share of changes in U.S. wage structure over recent decades.
- Limitation: U.S. historical evidence does not imply the same magnitude or distribution for generative AI.

### L03 — New tasks and reinstatement

- Source: Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, “Unpacking Skill Bias: Automation and New Tasks,” NBER Working Paper 26681, 2020.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w26681>
- Supports: new task creation can reinstate labor demand and should be analyzed separately from automation.
- Limitation: new tasks are not guaranteed to appear quickly enough or benefit displaced workers.

## AI evidence and current frontier structure

### A01 — Generative AI as a possible GPT

- Source: Flavio Calvino, Daniel Haerle, and Sarah Liu, “Is Generative AI a General Purpose Technology?,” OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 40, 2025.
- URL: <https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/is-generative-ai-a-general-purpose-technology_704e2d12-en.html>
- Supports: generative AI exhibits early evidence of pervasiveness, continuous improvement, and innovation spawning; complementary policy and adoption determine long-run productivity.
- Limitation: classification remains provisional and early productivity evidence is incomplete.

### A02 — Generative AI, productivity, innovation, and entrepreneurship

- Source: Flavio Calvino, Jelmer Reijerink, and Lea Samek, “The Effects of Generative AI on Productivity, Innovation and Entrepreneurship,” OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers, 2025.
- URL: <https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-effects-of-generative-ai-on-productivity-innovation-and-entrepreneurship_b21df222-en.html>
- Supports: AI can automate tasks, augment skills, reshape firms, and support innovation; outcomes depend on task and user context.
- Limitation: early experimental evidence cannot establish economy-wide long-run effects.

### A03 — 2026 AI research and development landscape

- Source: Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI, “Research and Development,” *2026 AI Index Report*.
- URL: <https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/research-and-development>
- Supports: AI R&D is globally distributed across industry, academia, states, open-source ecosystems, compute infrastructure, and talent; frontier transparency is declining while compute and open-source activity expand.
- Limitation: the report is descriptive and does not prove that any pacing regime must fail.

### A04 — 2026 AI technical performance

- Source: Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI, “Technical Performance,” *2026 AI Index Report*.
- URL: <https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/technical-performance>
- Supports: capability is improving rapidly, top models are converging, national performance gaps fluctuate, and benchmark reliability/gaming remain serious problems.
- Limitation: benchmark performance is not a complete measure of economic or strategic capability.

### A05 — AI and science

- Source: Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI, “Science,” *2026 AI Index Report*.
- URL: <https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/science>
- Supports: AI participation in scientific research is expanding, but end-to-end autonomous research remains substantially below expert performance and experimentally confirmed discoveries remain limited.
- Limitation: aggregate benchmark evidence may lag frontier private deployments.

### A06 — Corporate evidence on AI productivity

- Source: Salomé Baslandze et al., “Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives,” NBER Working Paper 34984, 2026.
- URL: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984>
- Supports: 2026 executive survey evidence reports heterogeneous but positive productivity gains, concentrated in some sectors and channels.
- Limitation: survey expectations and revenue-based productivity measures are not causal long-run welfare estimates.

### A07 — Pacing the Frontier statement

- Source: “Pacing the Frontier,” July 2026.
- URL: <https://www.pacingthefrontier.com/>
- Supports: 1,132 employees of frontier AI companies requested an international effort to build technical and governance tools for deliberately pacing automated AI-development progress; the statement explicitly cites competitive pressure against unilateral slowdown.
- Limitation: signatory status demonstrates concern and coordination demand, not feasibility, optimality, or proof of imminent intelligence explosion.

### A08 — Distinguishing AI races

- Source: Raghav Akula and Oliver Guest, “Unpacking AI Racing: Three Potential Objectives in International AI Competition,” SSRN, July 2026.
- URL: <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/7046799.pdf?abstractid=7046799&mirid=1&type=2>
- Supports: frontier capability, national adoption, and global diffusion are distinct strategic races with different metrics and policy implications.
- Limitation: very recent working paper; not mature consensus or causal evidence.

## Coordination counterexamples and verification

### C01 — Montreal Protocol

- Source: United Nations Environment Programme, “About the Montreal Protocol.”
- URL: <https://www.unep.org/ozonaction/who-we-are/about-montreal-protocol>
- Supports: near-universal international coordination is possible when controlled substances, reporting obligations, trade rules, timetables, assistance, and measurable commitments are defined.
- Limitation: ozone-depleting chemicals differ from a strategic, modular, knowledge-intensive cognition technology.

### C02 — Nuclear safeguards and verification

- Source: International Atomic Energy Agency, “Safeguards and Verification”; U.S. Department of Energy, “International Nuclear Safeguards.”
- URLs:
  - <https://data.iaea.org/group/about/safeguards-and-verification>
  - <https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/nuclear-nexus-international-nuclear-safeguards>
- Supports: international verification can combine declarations, material accounting, facility access, containment, surveillance, and monitoring around physically defined nuclear materials and facilities.
- Limitation: safeguards are incomplete, jurisdiction-dependent, and materially easier to anchor than distributed algorithmic and organizational knowledge production.


## Compute governance, auditing, and verification

### G01 — Computing power and AI governance

- Source: Girish Sastry et al., “Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence,” 2024.
- URL: <https://law-ai.org/computing-power-and-the-governance-of-artificial-intelligence/>
- Supports: advanced compute is comparatively detectable, excludable, quantifiable, and concentrated; it can provide visibility, allocation, and enforcement levers.
- Limitation: readiness varies substantially; compute does not measure all algorithmic, data, organizational, inference, or downstream capability progress, and poorly scoped governance can increase privacy and concentration risks.

### G02 — Training-compute thresholds

- Source: Lennart Heim and Leonie Koessler, “Training Compute Thresholds: Features and Functions in AI Regulation,” 2024.
- URL: <https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/training-compute-thresholds-features-and-functions-in-ai-regulation>
- Supports: training compute can serve as an externally verifiable initial filter for reporting and regulatory scrutiny.
- Limitation: compute is an imperfect proxy for risk and should not independently determine mitigation; thresholds require updating and capability evaluation.

### G03 — Verification of frontier-AI research restrictions

- Source: Aaron Scher, “Verifying Restrictions on Frontier AI Research,” arXiv:2606.28694, 2026.
- URL: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28694>
- Supports: proposed AI research restrictions have a non-empty verification design space, including infrastructure records, code review, inspections, intelligence, and whistleblowers.
- Limitation: many mechanisms are preliminary, intrusive, undesirable, or not implementation-ready; cataloguing mechanisms does not establish an effective treaty.

### G04 — Hardware-level AI compute governance

- Source: Samar Ansari, “Hardware-Level Governance of AI Compute: A Feasibility Taxonomy for Regulatory Compliance and Treaty Verification,” arXiv:2604.04712, 2026.
- URL: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04712>
- Supports: monitoring, verification, and enforcement mechanisms can be evaluated technically; some domestic mechanisms are feasible and treaty-grade mechanisms remain an active engineering agenda.
- Limitation: recent preprint; it reports that the mechanisms most valuable for treaty verification are among the least mature and face adversarial and sovereignty constraints.

### G05 — Frontier AI auditing

- Source: Miles Brundage et al., “Frontier AI Auditing: Toward Rigorous Third-Party Assessment of Safety and Security Practices at Leading AI Companies,” 2026.
- URL: <https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/frontier-ai-auditing-toward-rigorous-third-party-assessment-of-safety-and-security-practices-at-leading-ai-companies>
- Supports: deep-access third-party audits can evaluate organization-level safety, internal deployment, security, and decision processes beyond public model testing.
- Limitation: auditing can lag, become a checkbox exercise, depend on access and standards, and cannot alone cover non-participating states or hidden actors.

### G06 — Biological Weapons Convention verification limit

- Source: United Nations Office of Legal Affairs, “Convention on the Prohibition of Biological and Toxin Weapons,” historical note.
- URL: <https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/cpdpsbbtwd/cpdpsbbtwd.html>
- Supports: the Biological Weapons Convention prohibits a highly consequential dual-use domain but does not establish a dedicated verification regime comparable to IAEA safeguards.
- Limitation: biological-weapons governance is not identical to AI; lack of a verification regime does not prove prohibition has no normative or behavioral effect.

### G07 — Frontier capability and safeguard trends

- Source: UK AI Security Institute, “Frontier AI Trends Report,” 2025.
- URL: <https://www.aisi.gov.uk/frontier-ai-trends-report>
- Supports: frontier capabilities are improving rapidly across tested domains, strengthening the case for empirical monitoring, evaluation, and mitigation capacity.
- Limitation: evaluation trends do not establish a specific optimal governance regime or forecast exact future capability dates.
